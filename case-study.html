01 / Problem and opportunity

The bottleneck is the handoff from information to practice.

A source guide can explain a topic without giving a facilitator a ready-to-use exercise. Someone still needs to identify the audience, materials, activities, learning objectives, and review criteria, then assemble them into a consistent worksheet.

My problem framing focused on three possible costs: repeated assembly work, inconsistent artifacts, and review effort spent finding omissions. A faster draft is only valuable if it does not move that work downstream.

The job to be done“When I have a useful source guide, help me create an activity I can review and run, so I spend less effort assembling a document and more effort facilitating learning.”

Facilitator

Needs an editable starting point, not another long chat response.

Reviewer

Needs to see missing facts and judge whether the draft reflects its source.

Program owner

Needs repeatable quality and a credible cost-per-accepted-artifact story.

These are illustrative personas and problem hypotheses, not claims of completed interviews, customer research, or measured demand.

02 / My contribution and process

I worked backward from a useful deliverable.

I translated the desired worksheet into product requirements, separated what the system could draft from what a person must approve, and used AI-assisted implementation to make the public workflow inspectable.

  1. 01

    Define the output before choosing the interaction

    I made the worksheet's 17 fields a shared contract. That gave prompts, revisions, previews, and exports the same definition of a complete artifact.

    Product artifact: a field schema and section-level requirements.
  2. 02

    Set the boundaries of automation

    I separated content drafting, editing, presentation, and delivery. Unknown facts stay visible; source text is evidence, not authority to bypass the review process.

    Product artifact: prompt responsibilities and acceptance rules.
  3. 03

    Design the exception path alongside the happy path

    I treated missing source material, invalid output, and stale approval as product states. The public demo makes a failed source visible and requires new approval after an edit.

    Product artifact: a controlled review loop with explicit failure behavior.
  4. 04

    Inspect the artifact, not just the chat response

    I used source/output comparisons and focused checks to evaluate field preservation, readable activity outlines, and usable downloads. A polished response is not the same as a correct document.

    Product artifact: fictional examples and repeatable acceptance checks.
  5. 05

    Package the work for independent evaluation

    I kept the reusable low-code design and built a disconnected browser demo with original fictional content. Visitors can inspect the review and export experience without access to private systems.

    Product artifact: this site, a product case study, and configurable blueprints.

03 / Strategy and prioritization

A narrow, trustworthy loop before a broad platform.

In the public MVP

Prove one complete journey

Fictional source selection, a structured draft, manual revisions, review, and Word/JSON export. Make uncertainty and blocked actions visible.

Deliberately deferred

Avoid unproven breadth

Arbitrary document ingestion, live cloud connections, multi-user permissions, organizational analytics, and claims about learning outcomes.

The alternatives I considered in the product design

I prioritized decisions that reduce downstream uncertainty over extra features. The optional cloud design stays low-code to support iteration; the browser layer is a separate public demonstration, not a replacement production platform.

04 / Product and business metrics

Measure accepted work, not generated words.

Proposed north-star metric

Reviewer-accepted worksheets per active facilitator-week

Count a worksheet only after content review and a usable export. An active facilitator has started at least one worksheet that week. Count each worksheet once, not each revision.

Proposed pilot criteria—not measured results. The example thresholds below are planning choices, not historical performance or commitments. They would be agreed with pilot participants after establishing a comparable baseline.

MeasureDefinitionExample decision rule
Time to accepted worksheetMedian active authoring + review + correction time on matched tasks. Track elapsed lead time separately.Target at least 30% less active time than the manual baseline, without worse quality.
First-pass acceptanceFirst drafts accepted without substantive corrections / all first drafts reviewed.Proposed target 80% or higher; inspect rejection reasons by field.
Unsupported-content rateReviewed drafts with at least one unsupported material claim / all drafts audited.Any critical unsupported instruction pauses the pilot. Define severity before evaluation.
Usable export reliabilityAttempts producing a readable, content-faithful artifact within the agreed time / all generation attempts, including failures.Proposed target 95% or higher; a file existing alone is not success.
Repeat useActivated facilitators producing an accepted worksheet in the following week / activated facilitators observed for two full weeks. Activation means a first accepted worksheet.Measure first; segment by task frequency rather than invent an adoption claim.
Cost per accepted worksheetAuthoring and review labor, failed attempts, platform usage, support, and allocated setup cost / accepted worksheets.Improve against the manual baseline at the same quality bar; do not count labor capacity as cash savings.

A fast model response is not end-to-end time saved. A successful export is not proof of learning. None of these pilot metrics is collected by this public site.

05 / Business judgment

Make the assumptions inspectable.

This planning model asks whether reduced assembly effort could justify implementation and running costs. It charges for every assisted attempt and includes a full manual fallback when the assisted workflow is unsuccessful.

Illustrative assumptions only. These defaults are fictional planning numbers, not original project data, actual platform prices, measured savings, or a forecast. Change them to see how the argument holds up. Inputs remain local to this page.

Capacity value is the value of potentially reusable time, not revenue, profit, a staffing reduction, or cash saved. The payback figure is capacity-equivalent, not financial payback. Real decisions require observed quality, demand, costs, and a plan to reuse the time.

See the formulas and limits

Let V = monthly attempts, M = manual minutes, A = assisted minutes including review, s = assisted completion share, R = hourly labor value, C = usage cost per attempt, F = fixed monthly cost, and I = one-time setup cost.

  • Minutes with the workflow = A + (1 - s) × M. An unsuccessful attempt consumes the assisted time and then restarts manually.
  • Monthly hours freed = V × (s × M - A) / 60.
  • Net modeled capacity value = (hours freed × R) - (V × C + F).
  • Fixed-cost break-even volume = ceil(F / ((s × M - A) × R / 60 - C)), only when per-attempt contribution is positive.
  • Capacity-equivalent setup payback = I / net monthly capacity value, only when that monthly value is positive.

The model assumes comparable tasks, unchanged quality, stable costs and demand, and a reusable value for freed time. It does not model cash realization, taxes, discounting, ramp-up, retention, or learning outcomes. Put review and normal corrections into assisted time; put recurring support and licensing into fixed costs. Do not double-count them. Results are rounded for display; calculations use unrounded values.

What I learned from the product work

  • Start with the artifact. A concrete output contract makes scope, quality, and ownership easier to reason about.
  • More prose is not more value. A short, task-specific outline can be more useful than an impressive-looking wall of text.
  • Treat source availability as a product dependency. Better wording cannot repair missing evidence.
  • Trust includes saying “not ready.” Visible missing facts and blocked exports are useful behavior, not merely error handling.

07 / Validation and rollout plan

Earn the next stage with evidence.

  1. Validate the need. Speak with prospective facilitators and reviewers; observe comparable manual tasks, agree what “accepted” means, and establish the baseline. This research is proposed, not completed.
  2. Run a bounded pilot. Use consented, non-sensitive examples, matched tasks, counterbalanced task order, and the same review rubric. Include failures and manual restarts. Report sample size and uncertainty; a small pilot is not a production claim.
  3. Expand only if quality and economics hold. Verify any optional connected build, access controls, support ownership, and per-accepted-artifact cost. Pause on critical unsupported content or a broken review boundary before adding sources or users.

A future evaluation would record timestamps, revision counts, outcome categories, and reviewed quality scores with appropriate consent and retention rules—not raw source bodies by default. No such analytics or pilot is running on this site.

The product-management principle

Make the value hypothesis explicit, constrain what can go wrong, and distinguish a working demonstration from a proven business outcome.
