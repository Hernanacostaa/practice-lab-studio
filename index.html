Goodbye,
blank worksheet.

A manual is not a training exercise. Someone still has to turn all that information into something people can actually practice. I was doing that work—and started wondering how much of the repetitive part a tool could handle.

So I built one. Source documents become a structured first draft. Authors review, edit, and get a Word document they can use. Less worksheet wrangling. A person still in charge.

Global curriculum leadership

Great training shouldn’t need me in every room.

As a lead in global curriculum work, I turn hands-on engineering knowledge into guides and train-the-trainer programs. The best handoff is another team feeling ready to teach it themselves.

Here’s what I actually did

The challenge. Knowing how new technology works is different from helping another team learn, practice, and teach it.

My contribution. I worked alongside engineers and program managers, authored hands-on guides, designed one- and two-week learning programs, and helped trainers from different regions prepare to deliver locally. Field questions fed back into the guidance.

The lesson. A scalable program needs more than good content. It needs clear practice, a way to check readiness, and someone equipped to own the next delivery.

Mobility & new-technology readiness

New tech. Real people. Let’s get hands-on.

My mobility-team work brings technical learning closer to new-technology readiness. It’s a good reminder that a plan has to work around actual equipment, actual schedules, and actual people.

Here’s what I actually did

The challenge. A training plan has to work around real equipment, people, and operational constraints—not just look complete on paper.

My contribution. Alongside my training role, I took on a global rapid-response stretch assignment for new technology. I bring field experience, technical instruction, and reusable learning materials to that work.

The lesson. Stay close to the team doing the job. The gap between a documented process and the experience of using it is often where the next improvement begins.

Self-initiated AI product building

“What if we built a tool for that?”

I started building AI content tools alongside my regular job, then put them in front of other teams. Making a demo work was exciting. Making the output worth using was the more interesting challenge.

Here’s what I actually did

The challenge. Learning teams repeatedly turn technical source material into exercises, readiness assessments, and other training content.

My contribution. I built the working agent workflow, designed prompts and input/output contracts, evaluated outputs against learning needs, and iterated on review and document delivery. Practice Lab Studio makes one part of that thinking publicly inspectable.

The lesson. A compelling demo is a beginning. Source quality, failure behavior, human review, and the handoff into someone’s workflow matter just as much.
